## Problem

Operational ticket triage usually depends on manual reading or an opaque
classifier: no structured decision, no audit trail, and no honest measurement
of where automation is right and where it needs human review.

Ops Triage AI tackles this problem within clear bounds: it receives tickets
and produces structured classification (category, priority, risk, suggested
team) plus an auditable decision about what can proceed automatically and
what requires human review. The repository is public, so every decision
described here can be inspected in the code.

## Architecture

The system's main flow:

**HTTP → validation → PersistedTriageService → TriageTicket →
DeterministicTriageClassifier → OllamaTriageClassifier → HybridPolicy →
TriageDecision → Prisma/PostgreSQL → Feedback/Audit Trail.**

Each piece has an explicit responsibility:

- **DeterministicTriageClassifier:** a predictable, cheap, reproducible
  baseline with heuristic signals and confidence.
- **OllamaTriageClassifier:** semantic interpretation with a local LLM
  (qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q5_K_S) and validated structured output.
- **HybridPolicy:** decides human review, fallback, and decision source. It
  is a pure decision layer — no field-level merging between classifiers.
- **PersistedTriageService:** orchestrates the persisted lifecycle: IDs,
  runs, decisions, and feedback.
- **Persistence:** Ticket → TriageRun → TriageDecision → Feedback, with
  append-only feedback — the original decision is never overwritten.

## Engineering decisions

- The deterministic classifier is the guardrail and comparison signal; the
  LLM is the semantic classifier. Both receive the same ticket and are not
  treated as statistically independent.
- Known LLM failures (timeout, unavailability, invalid response) produce a
  deterministic fallback decision, always with human review. Unknown failures
  keep propagating — graceful degradation without claiming the results are
  equivalent.
- Security and operations from the start: API key, body and concurrency
  limits, X-Request-Id, redacted JSON logs, `/metrics`, `/health`, `/ready`,
  graceful shutdown, and abandoned-run reconciliation.

## Evaluation

The official evaluation is a frozen held-out benchmark: 70 synthetic
tickets, a single run, no rule tuning against held-out errors. Frozen
parameters — commit `f36e8ef8fcc70be09ab5ba9883efc7c34f3591a2`, prompt
`ollama-triage-v3`, model `qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q5_K_S`, 120000ms timeout.

The official results table below belongs to the historical deterministic,
Ollama, and `HybridPolicy` benchmark. A later, isolated evaluation added Jev
1.13 as a third paradigm on the same frozen held-out set; Jev was not
integrated into `HybridPolicy`. Laya was evaluated afterward, first zero-shot
and then with domain adaptation, also outside the runtime.

The harness compares the deterministic baseline, the standalone LLM, and the
hybrid on quality, severity recall, disagreement, fallback, review reasons,
and latency. It deliberately does not emit: human-review precision/recall
(the held-out set has no independent ground truth for it), suggested-team
correctness, standalone LLM latency, or separate persistence/HTTP latency.

## Official results

|                        Metric | Deterministic |         LLM |      Hybrid |
| ----------------------------: | ------------: | ----------: | ----------: |
|             Category accuracy |        0.8286 |      0.9571 |      0.9571 |
|             Category macro-F1 |        0.8512 |      0.9550 | not emitted |
|             Priority accuracy |        0.9000 |      0.9143 |      0.9143 |
|                 Risk accuracy |        0.9571 |      0.9143 |      0.9143 |
| HIGH/CRITICAL priority recall |        0.7857 |      1.0000 |      1.0000 |
|              HIGH risk recall |        0.5714 |      0.7143 |      0.7143 |
|          Exact tuple accuracy |   not emitted | not emitted |      0.8286 |

Operationally on the held-out set: 0.5143 review rate (severity,
disagreement, and confidence signals), 0.3286 baseline/LLM disagreement, 0
fallback, no LLM failures, 70/70 decisions with hybrid source. Hybrid
latency: p50 6257.7ms, p95 7078.6ms, max 7556.9ms.

On a frozen 70-ticket held-out benchmark, the local LLM improved category
accuracy from 82.9% to 95.7% and HIGH/CRITICAL priority recall from 78.6% to
100%, while the deterministic baseline retained higher overall risk accuracy
(95.7% vs 91.4%). The hybrid policy preserved the LLM's classification
quality while routing 51.4% of cases to human review based on severity,
disagreement, and confidence signals.

## Trade-offs

- The hybrid preserves LLM quality and pays for it with human review on more
  than half the cases plus 6-to-7-second latency — fit for async triage, not
  for a latency-critical synchronous path.
- The baseline won on overall risk accuracy (95.71% vs 91.43%): the LLM was
  not better at everything, and the regression is published above.
- An adversarial DEV baseline (out of distribution) dropped the
  deterministic classifier to 0.5 category, 0.5 priority, and 0.75 risk
  accuracy — evidence that explicit rules are brittle outside known
  vocabulary, which justifies combining with the LLM instead of a straight
  swap.

## Limits of the historical hybrid benchmark

Limits of this historical evaluation:

- Synthetic English-only dataset with 70 examples.
- A single local model evaluated.
- One official run — no LLM variance measured.
- Confidence is heuristic, not calibrated probability.
- The held-out set has no independent ground truth for human review.
- Review rate is not precision/recall.
- Suggested-team correctness was not emitted.
- Standalone LLM latency was not emitted.
- Separate persistence/HTTP latency was not emitted.
- The system was not validated under real production traffic.
- The baseline had higher overall risk accuracy.
- Hybrid latency landed at roughly 6 to 7 seconds.
- The adversarial DEV baseline showed 0.5 category accuracy, 0.5 priority
  accuracy, and 0.75 risk accuracy.

## Jev 1.13 experiment

This experiment evaluated a third paradigm against the same taxonomy:
deterministic rules, a generative LLM, and a typed probabilistic decision
model. It was an isolated evaluation: Jev was not integrated into
`HybridPolicy` and did not replace Ollama.

The `TriageClassifier` adapter used Bun native fetch and the OpenRouter
Decisions API with `typesafe/jev-1.13`; all 73 real responses resolved to
`typesafe/jev-1.13-20260917`. No dependency was added. Three DEV calls ran
before the freeze (`1b6d7ff`); commit `4c41e0b` froze the configuration and ran
one held-out evaluation on the same 70 synthetic labels used in the historical
comparison.

| Metric                        | Deterministic | Ollama | Jev 1.13 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------: | -----: | -------: |
| Category accuracy             |        0.8286 | 0.9571 |   1.0000 |
| Category macro-F1             |        0.8512 | 0.9550 |   1.0000 |
| Priority accuracy             |        0.9000 | 0.9143 |   0.9857 |
| Risk accuracy                 |        0.9571 | 0.9143 |   0.9571 |
| HIGH/CRITICAL priority recall |        0.7857 | 1.0000 |   1.0000 |
| HIGH risk recall              |        0.5714 | 0.7143 |   0.8571 |

Standalone Jev got the category/priority/risk tuple exactly right in 66/70
cases (0.9429). The historical benchmark did not emit this metric for the
standalone deterministic and Ollama classifiers; the 0.8286 exact tuple
accuracy above belongs to the historical hybrid path, which has a different
decision flow.

Across Jev's 70 held-out calls, standalone latency averaged 569.4 ms, with a
545.5 ms p50, 712.2 ms p95, and 1,142.2 ms maximum. The API reported 90,229
input tokens and US$ 0.003789618 in cost; there were no API or schema failures.
The historical Ollama benchmark did not emit standalone latency or cost. Its
6–7 second latency belongs to the hybrid path and is not a like-for-like
comparison with this standalone Jev call.

Jev retained probability distributions, continuous confidence, and top-1 /
top-2 margins. Still, one incorrectly classified HIGH risk had confidence
0.86: high confidence does not guarantee semantic correctness.

### Selective automation and calibration analysis

The exploratory analysis below requires all three confidence values to meet
the threshold. These are observed results on the same small dataset, not a
production threshold choice:

| Threshold | Coverage | Exact accuracy on covered subset |
| --------: | -------: | -------------------------------: |
|      0.00 |    70/70 |                           94.29% |
|      0.50 |    65/70 |                           95.38% |
|      0.70 |    60/70 |                           96.67% |
|      0.80 |    54/70 |                             100% |
|      0.90 |    51/70 |                             100% |
|      0.95 |    45/70 |                             100% |
|      0.99 |    37/70 |                             100% |

Exploratory calibration analysis measured Brier scores of 0.0005 for
category, 0.0386 for priority, and 0.0800 for risk; top-1 ECE was 0.0054,
0.0329, and 0.0226, respectively. These are descriptive estimates from 70
synthetic tickets with sparse bins; they do not establish calibration for this
domain.

On the same frozen synthetic held-out labels, Jev produced the strongest
measured category and priority results in this experiment, along with typed
probability distributions and sub-second average standalone latency. The
small synthetic dataset does not establish production superiority. The
architectural decision remains benchmark/evaluation only; there was no
`HybridPolicy` integration.

## Laya: from zero-shot to domain adaptation

After Jev, I evaluated the general `convaiinnovations/laya` checkpoint, a
non-autoregressive decision model with code and open weights under Apache 2.0.
I used `laya==0.3.23` and the protocol’s frozen revision, retaining the same
held-out set. This base checkpoint differs from `laya-typed-decisions`, which
has already been specialized on other workflows.

Zero-shot, Laya got **8/70 tuples right (11.43%)**. The clearest pattern was
**40/40 expected LOW priorities predicted as MEDIUM**. Auditing options,
indices, mapping, normalization, and parsed outputs found no integration bug
that invalidated the behavior. The next hypothesis was that the general model
needed adaptation to our taxonomy and domain.

### Adaptation protocol

I separated **1,120 TRAIN tickets** for changing weights and **280 VALIDATION
tickets** for checkpoint selection. The 70 HELD-OUT tickets stayed outside
data generation, training, validation, selection, tuning, thresholds, and
calibration. TRAIN and VALIDATION are synthetic too, sharing scenario families
with distinct wording.

Fine-tuning started from the same base model. VALIDATION selected epoch 4 by
highest exact-tuple accuracy, using predeclared tie-breakers. **Freeze 2** fixed
data, weights, code, and configuration before the single official adapted
evaluation. The sequence is baseline → Ollama → HybridPolicy → separate Jev
evaluation → Laya zero-shot → adaptation → selection → Freeze 2 → adapted held-out.

### Training infrastructure

The inference host, with four vCPUs and roughly 4 GB RAM, was unsuitable for
this training. I used a temporary RunPod GPU: **NVIDIA RTX A5000, 24 GB VRAM**,
Python 3.11, PyTorch 2.5.1, and CUDA 12.1. Training ran for four epochs with
mixed precision (FP16 autocast / FP32 master weights), gradient accumulation,
and gradient checkpointing to manage memory.

In the first run, the scheduler advanced even when `GradScaler` skipped an
optimizer update. I preserved the artifacts, rejected the run on methodological
validity, and corrected the coordination before repeating training and opening
the adapted held-out evaluation. The official run recorded **420 attempts,
413 applied updates, seven skips, and 413 scheduler steps**. It was not chosen
for more favorable accuracy.

The `.safetensors` checkpoint was retained locally outside the Pod; SHA-256,
size, selection, and configuration are documented in public artifacts. Adapted
weights were not published. This cycle supplies my first hands-on evidence in
**ML Systems / AI Infrastructure**, bounded to training, checkpoints, and
experimental evaluation.

Final evaluation ran on CPU without the GPU, averaging **1,619 ms** per ticket,
versus 1,414 ms zero-shot. Jev had recorded 569 ms over API/network in a
different context. Local inference did not automatically mean lower latency
on this CPU; the serving paths do not support a like-for-like speed comparison.

Measured GPU tasks totaled 1,523.51 seconds. At the recorded rate of roughly
US$ 0.28/hour including disk, **US$ 0.1185 estimates those tasks**, excluding
setup and idle time; it is not the Pod’s total bill. Laya incurred US$ 0 in
external per-token/request charges, while infrastructure and operations remained
our responsibility. The report does not document teardown: the Pod had not
been destroyed when evaluation was completed.

### Results and severity regression

Accuracy by field and exact tuple, on the same held-out set:

| Metric      | Zero-shot | Adapted |
| ----------- | --------: | ------: |
| Category    |    84.29% |  97.14% |
| Priority    |    31.43% |  94.29% |
| Risk        |    62.86% |  92.86% |
| Exact tuple |    11.43% |  85.71% |

Correct tuples rose from **8/70 to 60/70**, and LOW → MEDIUM errors fell from
**40/40 to 0/40**. HIGH risk recall rose from **57.14% to 100%** (4/7 → 7/7),
but HIGH/CRITICAL priority recall regressed from **92.86% to 85.71%**
(13/14 → 12/14). Aggregate improvement did not eliminate severity errors.

Jev had reached **66/70 correct tuples (94.29%) without any of our TRAIN
examples**; adapted Laya received 1,120. The comparison is asymmetric: Jev
showed strong out-of-the-box generalization on this benchmark, while Laya
showed the control open weights offer for adapting behavior to a domain.

### Confidence and generalization limits

Adapted Laya’s confidence stayed close to 1, yet **10/70 tuples were still
wrong**. Thresholds through 0.90 retained every ticket; 0.95 and 0.99 removed
only one, leaving exact-tuple accuracy below 90%. High confidence did not
become a reliable estimate of correctness, and its semantics differ from
Jev’s reported confidence.

Exact-tuple accuracy was **100% on TRAIN → 92.50% on VALIDATION → 85.71% on
HELD-OUT**. Strong fit and saturated confidence are signs consistent with
memorization and overconfidence; this small sample does not establish how
severe the effect is.

These evaluations use English, a single domain, and synthetic data, with one
official run per protocol and no validation on real traffic. **Jev and Laya
remain evaluation-only, outside HybridPolicy and the runtime.** The experiment
exercised evaluation and adaptation, without deploying an adapted production
service.
